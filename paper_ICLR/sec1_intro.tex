\section{Introduction}

A sensor stream that runs unattended almost always carries two things at once. A wearable accelerometer records both what the wearer is doing and how the device happens to be shaking in this instant. The quantity anyone acts on is the first --- the state that lasts --- and the second changes far faster than any decision taken from it. This is the ordinary situation in condition monitoring, activity recognition, sleep staging and health-state estimation, and it is why the two are worth keeping in separate coordinates. A named block buys three things: interpretation gets a fixed address rather than a post-hoc story, the price of using only that block becomes a measurable number, and scarce labels can be spent on a narrow subspace instead of the whole embedding.

\paragraph{Why the separation has to happen during training.}
An embedding can always be rotated afterwards, and unsupervised unmixings --- principal component analysis, slow feature analysis (SFA), independent component analysis --- do exactly that at a fraction of the cost, so this is the sharpest objection to anything done at training time. A post-hoc method must rank directions by a quantity computable from a frozen embedding: variance, slowness, non-Gaussianity, or predictability under a linear autoregressive model \citep{richthofer2013}. \textbf{None of those is persistence.} The horizon is not a property of the embedding at all but of the predictive objective: free while training, and gone once training ends. Section~\ref{sec:rotation} measures what that buys and what it does not --- where the surrogate and persistence agree, which is the ordinary case on real data, post-hoc rotation is as good or better.

\paragraph{Persistence is not confined to the slow band.}
Separating ``slow'' from ``fast'' can mean separating the \emph{signal}, decomposing a waveform into trend and fluctuation \citep{cleveland1990, huang1998}, or separating the \emph{factors} that generated it \citep{hyvarinen2016}; the two readings are routinely treated as one, and they diverge in exactly one direction. Calling a factor \emph{persistent} if it still contributes to prediction $\Delta$ steps ahead, the slow part of the signal is necessarily persistent, since a component confined to low frequencies has slowly decaying autocorrelation \citep{khinchin1934} and remaining autocorrelation is a contribution to distant prediction --- but the converse fails, because low-pass filtering is linear in the signal while a factor may ride on a nonlinear statistic of it. Engineering practice knows this: in rolling-element bearing diagnostics the fault frequencies do not stand out in the spectrum of the raw vibration and become apparent only after demodulation, in the spectrum of the Hilbert envelope \citep{randall2011}. Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} shows what such a factor costs a slowness criterion.

\paragraph{One criterion covers both kinds.}
So a method keyed to the slow band reaches persistent-and-slow factors and misses persistent-but-not-slow ones. What both kinds share is the property they were defined by: \textbf{both remain useful for predicting the distant future, and transient factors do not.} That criterion is controlled by a variable prediction-based learning already contains --- the horizon $\Delta$, since pushing it out leaves only long-persisting factors useful \citep{bialek2001, creutzig2008} --- and nothing in that argument refers to which band the factor occupies. We gate that axis. HGLP places a single soft threshold on the horizon: beyond it the predictor may no longer read a designated block, so the only remaining route to lowering long-range loss is to write the current state into the block that is left. The complement $\zmix$ is left unconstrained and keeps both factors (Section~\ref{sec:gate}).

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) An inductive bias that gates an axis already present in any predictive learner, splitting the persistent factor into a designatable block, together with a criterion that sets the one coefficient governing that split \emph{without task labels}. (ii) An evaluation protocol that scores the separation from both sides --- a block $\times$ factor matrix --- and a single summarizing index, SEP. (iii) Measurements on synthetic data, PTB-XL and HAPT, including an audit of where the method fails. The results cut both ways. On synthetic data we lead label-free post-hoc rotation on both loss families (SEP \numSynthOurs{} against SFA's \numSynthSfa); on real data we trail SFA in three of four settings by \numRealLossLo--\numRealLossHi. Splitting the index into its three terms locates that shortfall entirely on \emph{exclusion}, the one term our objective cannot enforce, and replacing the linear probe with a small neural one --- given equally to every baseline --- collapses that same term and no other. The separation also costs $-0.111$ macro-F1 downstream on HAPT.
