\section{Introduction}

A sensor stream that runs unattended almost always carries two things at once. A wrist or waist accelerometer records both what the wearer is doing and how the device happens to be shaking in this instant; an overnight EEG records both the sleep stage and the momentary waveform; a machine's vibration records both its accumulated wear and the load of the current second. In each pair the quantity anyone acts on is the first --- the state that lasts --- and the second changes far faster than any decision taken from it. This is the ordinary situation in unsupervised condition monitoring, human activity recognition, sleep staging and health-state estimation, and it is why we want a representation in which the two are not stored in the same coordinates.

Keeping them apart buys three concrete things. The block that is supposed to hold the state can be named before any probe is fitted, so interpretation has a fixed address rather than a post-hoc story. The price of using only that block becomes a single measurable number instead of an unknown. And where labels are scarce, they can be spent on a narrow designated block rather than on the whole embedding. We measure the second and third of these and report the outcome plainly; it is mixed, and Section~\ref{sec:cost} gives it in full.

\paragraph{Why the separation has to happen during training.}
An embedding can always be rotated afterwards, and unsupervised unmixings --- principal component analysis, slow feature analysis (SFA), independent component analysis --- do exactly that at a fraction of the cost. This is the sharpest objection to anything done at training time, so we state our answer at the outset. A post-hoc method must rank directions by some quantity computable from a frozen embedding: variance, slowness, non-Gaussianity, or predictability under a linear autoregressive model \citep{richthofer2013}. \textbf{None of those is persistence.} Each stands in for it, and the substitution stays invisible until a dataset arrives on which the surrogate and persistence disagree --- on the synthetic data of Fig.~\ref{fig:persistence}, where the persistent factor is placed deliberately outside the slow band, a variance ranking fails outright (SEP \numSynthPca) and ordering directions by slowness recovers the persistent factor no better than ordering them at random. The prediction horizon is not a property of the embedding at all. It is a property of the predictive objective: free while training, gone once training ends.

A second difference is structural. A rotation conserves --- it can move information between coordinates but cannot change how much of it the encoder represents at all --- whereas training can. Our complement block recovers the transient factor better than SFA's complement in \numAllocLead{} of six settings, by as much as \numAllocMax, which no rebasing of SFA's own embedding could produce. Practically, a fitted unmixing is also a second artifact that travels with the model and is refitted whenever the deployment distribution moves, while a block index costs nothing at inference.

The counterweight belongs here rather than in a footnote. Where the surrogate and persistence do agree, post-hoc rotation is as good or better, and on both of our real datasets they agree --- which is why SFA leads us there. Appendix~\ref{app:coincidence} measures in what sense that agreement holds, and finds it is not even the same sense on the two datasets.

\paragraph{Persistence is not confined to the slow band.}
Separating ``slow'' from ``fast'' in a time series can mean two different things. One is separating the \emph{signal}, decomposing an observed waveform into a trend and a fluctuation, for which long-established tools exist \citep{cleveland1990, huang1998}. The other is separating the \emph{factors} that generated it \citep{hyvarinen2016} --- a patient's diagnosis, or the fact that a person is walking. The two are routinely treated as one, and they diverge in exactly one direction. We fix the term first: a factor is \emph{persistent} if it still contributes to prediction $\Delta$ steps ahead. Under that definition the slow part of the signal is necessarily persistent, since a component confined to low frequencies has slowly decaying autocorrelation \citep{khinchin1934} and remaining autocorrelation is a contribution to prediction at distant horizons. The converse fails, because low-pass filtering is linear in the signal while a factor may ride on a nonlinear statistic of it such as frequency or variance.

Engineering practice has long known this. In rolling-element bearing diagnostics the fault frequencies do not stand out in the spectrum of the raw vibration; they become apparent only after the signal is demodulated and the spectrum of its Hilbert envelope is read \citep{randall2011}. The same shape appears in ordinary frequency-modulated radio, where the message lives in the instantaneous frequency of a fast carrier and no low-pass of the raw waveform recovers it. Our synthetic generator is built on that second shape deliberately: the persistent factor sets the carrier frequency, so a low-pass reading returns the \emph{transient} factor ($R^2 = 0.90$) and essentially nothing of the persistent one ($R^2 < 10^{-5}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:persistence}). This is an existence claim --- such factors can exist --- and not a claim about how often they arise; how common they are in natural data this paper does not answer.

\paragraph{One criterion covers both kinds.}
So there are persistent factors that are also slow, and persistent factors that are not slow at all, and a method that targets only the slow band reaches one kind and misses the other. What the two kinds share is the property we defined them by: \textbf{both are still useful for predicting the distant future, and the transient factor is not.} Persistence is therefore controlled by a variable that prediction-based representation learning already contains --- the horizon $\Delta$. Pushing $\Delta$ farther out leaves only long-persisting factors useful \citep{bialek2001, creutzig2008}, and nothing about that argument refers to which band the factor occupies.

We gate that axis. HGLP places a single soft threshold on the horizon: beyond it the predictor may no longer read a designated block of coordinates, so the only remaining route to lowering the loss at long range is to write the current state into the block that is left. Because the latent space is rotation-symmetric, the persistent factor can be read out of any 16 dimensions, and our claim is therefore not that it \emph{gathers} in $\zslow$ but that the transient factor is \emph{emptied out} of it. The complement $\zmix$ is left unconstrained and keeps both factors; Section~\ref{sec:asymmetry} says why that one-sidedness is the design rather than an omission.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) An inductive bias that gates an axis already present in any predictive learner, the prediction horizon, splitting the persistent factor into a designatable block --- and a criterion that selects the one hyperparameter governing that split \emph{without task labels}. (ii) An evaluation protocol that scores the separation from both sides, a block $\times$ factor matrix, together with a single summarizing index, SEP. (iii) Measurements on synthetic data, PTB-XL and HAPT, including an audit of where the method fails.

The results cut both ways, and we report them that way. On synthetic data built so that persistence and slowness disagree we lead label-free post-hoc rotation on both loss families (SEP \numSynthOurs{} against SFA's \numSynthSfa). On real data we trail SFA in three of four settings, by \numRealLossLo--\numRealLossHi. Splitting the index into its three terms locates that shortfall entirely on \emph{exclusion} --- the one term our objective provably cannot enforce --- and replacing the linear probe with a small neural one, given equally to every baseline, collapses that same term and no other. The separation also carries a downstream cost, $-0.111$ macro-F1 on HAPT, which we quantify rather than omit.
